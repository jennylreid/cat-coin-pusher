## Tower rework, step 1 (Nov 20): why towers slosh and float, and options. Investigation only, nothing built or published
Measured on UAT 134's source in the real game, with a bot dropping 2 coins a stroke.

**Why they slosh and float**
1. **Giant towers stand on the pusher deck.** They ride it forward and back about 70 units on every stroke. In 30 s a tower
   travelled about 1,300 units but ended only 30 from where it started. That's the "wave pool". Once a tower is let go, its
   loose pieces and coins slide about on the deck, which is slippery (friction 0.05).
2. **Every tower in the game is made light on purpose.** Its density is 0.6, while coins are 1.5 (the code says "light, so the
   coins shove it along"), so a 7-coin tower weighs less than 3 coins. It also stands on the table on ice: the table's
   friction is 0.035, and the lower of the two values wins. A small push glides it, and it bobs about.
3. **Falls are slow-motion.** Gravity is 30 in a world roughly 20 times real coin size, so falls look about 2.5 times too slow.
   Every coin also has air drag of 0.8. Two seconds after the last drop, everything gets drag 4 ("calm", like honey), so a
   tower that spills then floats down and "alights".
4. **Quake lifts and pushes every piece evenly at its centre.** A tower hops and slides as one block instead of rocking.
   - Main board: Quake never spilled a tower.
   - Giant board: the tower's hold lets go on a hard knock, so in my test Quake did spill them (26 s). That may not happen
     every time on the phone.
5. **Main-board towers (Levels 2 and 4–10) are 4–12 coins**, about as wide as they are tall. They slide rather than topple:
   none spilled in 30 s, Quake included.

## What made the towers spill
Every tower in the game is one solid column (a stack of thin coins can't balance in the physics engine). These towers are
stacks of short columns instead, so they can buckle and pour. Four things had to be fixed along the way:
1. **Loose segments fall over by themselves.** On the moving deck, a 30-coin stack of loose segments rocks a little more on
   every stroke. In tests, a tower came down on its own within 12 s in 4 of 6 boards. So a standing tower holds together: its
   segments and top prize keep their place relative to the base.
   - The tower is let go to plain physics when any of these happens:
     - its base has slid forward along the deck by 2–5 units (pushed by the scraped coins);
     - its base runs off the deck's front edge;
     - something knocks a segment hard;
     - a segment breaks.
   - Once let go, it is given a forward turn over its base, falls over the front of the deck and comes apart.
2. **The game's tip rule misfired.** The game tips a tower once it has been "shoved" a few units, and riding on the deck counts
   as a shove, so every segment got kicked in the first second. That rule is off for these segments.
3. **The deck is slippery.** Its friction is 0.05, so towers drifted on the stroke's starts and stops. A tower base now grips the
   deck (friction 1.2) while it stands, and the grip goes back to normal once the tower is let go.
4. **Spills come apart like Board 3's.** A segment breaks at 11° (normal towers: 14°) into the Board 3 cascade: a wide, spinning
   spill.

The CP-37 slide (coins above the chin line are pushed forward) no longer knocks the tower-top prizes off, because a held tower
holds its prize. The frozen pickup and all other boards are unchanged.
